# Research Methodology and Evidence Framework

## Methodological Standard for Responsible AI Grant Research

**Version:** 1.0.0  
**Document ID:** WIBC-CRAI-002  
**Status:** Foundational Methodology  
**Maintained by:** Women in Blockchain Canada

## Purpose

This document defines the research methodology used to develop, evaluate, validate, and maintain the Women in Blockchain Canada responsible AI, blockchain, Web4, grant strategy, and innovation research repository.

The methodology supports the ten-volume AI Grant Strategy Series and the associated datasets, standards mappings, policy analyses, funding records, evaluation rubrics, knowledge graphs, and implementation frameworks.

Its purpose is to ensure that repository outputs are:

- evidence-based;
- reproducible;
- traceable;
- transparent;
- machine-readable;
- independently reviewable;
- suitable for scholarly citation;
- adaptable across jurisdictions.

## Methodological Design

The repository uses a modified mixed-methods, Delphi-inspired research framework.

The methodology combines:

1. structured literature review;
2. primary-source policy analysis;
3. grant-program analysis;
4. comparative standards mapping;
5. qualitative thematic analysis;
6. quantitative scoring;
7. stakeholder-informed evaluation;
8. systems thinking;
9. scenario analysis;
10. structured LLM-assisted synthesis;
11. convergence and divergence analysis;
12. final human judgment.

The framework is Delphi-inspired because it uses iterative evaluation, structured comparison, weighted criteria, reconsideration, and consensus testing.

It is not represented as a traditional anonymous expert Delphi study unless a formally recruited expert panel is documented.

## Research Questions

Each research artifact should identify one or more primary research questions.

Examples include:

- Which funding opportunities best align with a responsible AI initiative?
- What evidence is required to demonstrate research readiness?
- Which partnerships are necessary for successful implementation?
- How should responsible AI risks be assessed?
- What governance structures support trustworthy deployment?
- How can Canadian frameworks be adapted internationally?
- Which standards are relevant to a proposed project?
- How should public value and long-term impact be measured?

## Evidence Collection

Evidence should be collected from documented and identifiable sources.

Preferred sources include:

- official government websites;
- grant-program documentation;
- legislation and regulations;
- standards organizations;
- peer-reviewed publications;
- university publications;
- institutional research reports;
- public funding databases;
- audited organizational records;
- documented implementation results;
- recognized international organizations.

Secondary commentary may be used for context but should not replace primary evidence when primary sources are available.

## Evidence Hierarchy

Evidence should be classified using the following hierarchy.

### Level 1 — Primary Authoritative Evidence

Includes:

- legislation;
- regulations;
- official standards;
- government program documents;
- formal funding guidelines;
- court decisions;
- official institutional records;
- original datasets;
- signed agreements.

### Level 2 — Peer-Reviewed Research

Includes:

- peer-reviewed journal articles;
- peer-reviewed conference papers;
- academic books;
- formally reviewed research reports.

### Level 3 — Institutional Research

Includes:

- university reports;
- government studies;
- standards-body publications;
- international organization reports;
- recognized research institute publications.

### Level 4 — Verified Implementation Evidence

Includes:

- pilot results;
- technical documentation;
- audited project records;
- deployment reports;
- verified case studies;
- public repositories;
- reproducible demonstrations.

### Level 5 — Structured Stakeholder Evidence

Includes:

- interviews;
- focus groups;
- workshops;
- surveys;
- advisory-panel findings;
- documented community consultations.

### Level 6 — Reputable Industry Evidence

Includes:

- industry reports;
- technical white papers;
- market analyses;
- professional association publications.

### Level 7 — Expert Interpretation

Includes:

- documented professional judgment;
- specialist commentary;
- structured reviewer assessment.

### Level 8 — LLM-Assisted Analysis

Includes:

- summarization;
- classification;
- comparison;
- scenario generation;
- rubric testing;
- drafting assistance;
- semantic clustering.

LLM-generated output must be verified against source evidence before it is treated as a research finding.

## Source Evaluation Criteria

Each source should be assessed for:

- authority;

- currency;

- methodological quality;
- independence;

- jurisdictional applicability;
- potential conflicts of interest.

## Inclusion Criteria

A source may be included when it:

- directly addresses the research question;
- provides credible supporting evidence;
- contains relevant policy or program information;
- documents a recognized framework or standard;
- contributes to methodological comparison;
- provides verified implementation evidence;
- supports reproducibility or validation.

## Exclusion Criteria

A source may be excluded when it:

- cannot be verified;
- lacks identifiable authorship or provenance;
- duplicates stronger evidence;
- is materially outdated;
- presents unsupported promotional claims;
- misrepresents a standard or regulation;
- contains unverifiable AI-generated content;
- is outside the defined research scope.

## Delphi-Inspired Evaluation Process

The standard evaluation process uses iterative rounds.

### Round 1 — Evidence Assembly

Researchers collect and organize primary evidence, supporting literature, program requirements, and contextual information.

### Round 2 — Independent Assessment

Each opportunity, framework, or proposal is evaluated against predefined criteria.

### Round 3 — Comparative Scoring

Scores are compared across opportunities, reviewers, models, or scenarios.

### Round 4 — Divergence Review

Major disagreements, uncertainties, missing evidence, and methodological weaknesses are identified.

### Round 5 — Reassessment

Evaluators reconsider scores after reviewing the evidence and identified disagreements.

### Round 6 — Convergence Analysis

The process determines where judgments converge and where material disagreement remains.

### Round 7 — Final Human Determination

A designated human researcher or governance body makes the final determination and records the rationale.

## Weighted Evaluation Model

Research artifacts may use weighted criteria such as:

- strategic alignment;
- eligibility;
- funding fit;
- research quality;
- responsible AI alignment;
- partnership strength;
- implementation readiness;
- technical feasibility;
- governance maturity;
- public benefit;
- commercialization potential;
- scalability;
- knowledge contribution;
- risk exposure;
- sustainability.

Weights must be documented and should total 100 percent.

## Scoring Scale

A standard five-point scoring scale may be used.

| Score | Interpretation |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Very weak or unsupported |
| 2 | Limited alignment or evidence |
| 3 | Moderate alignment |
| 4 | Strong alignment |
| 5 | Exceptional alignment |

Scores should be supported by written evidence notes.

## Confidence Classification

Each major conclusion should receive a confidence classification.

| Confidence | Meaning |
|---|---|
| High | Supported by multiple authoritative sources |
| Moderate | Supported by credible but incomplete evidence |
| Low | Significant uncertainty or limited evidence |
| Provisional | Requires further verification |

## Standards Crosswalk Method

Standards mappings should distinguish between:

- direct equivalence;
- partial alignment;
- complementary requirements;
- conceptual similarity;
- implementation guidance;
- unresolved differences.

A crosswalk must not imply formal adoption, endorsement, or direct influence without documentary evidence.

## Validation Standard

Claims of validation should be categorized carefully.

### Independent Validation

A third party directly tests or confirms the framework.

### Conceptual Validation

External research independently supports similar principles or methods.

### Implementation Validation

The framework is successfully applied in a documented project.

### Standards Alignment

The framework maps to recognized standards or regulatory requirements.

### Historical Precedence

The framework predates later publications containing similar concepts.

Historical precedence alone does not prove direct influence or adoption.

## Limitations

The methodology recognizes that:

- grant programs change;
- regulations evolve;
- standards are revised;
- program eligibility may depend on unpublished interpretation;
- institutional capacity varies;
- LLM outputs may contain errors;
- public datasets may be incomplete;
- expert judgments may differ;
- scoring models may reflect selected priorities.

These limitations must be disclosed rather than concealed.
