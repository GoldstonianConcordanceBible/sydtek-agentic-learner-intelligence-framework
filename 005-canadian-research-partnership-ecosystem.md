# Canadian Research Partnership Ecosystem

## Collaboration Framework for Responsible AI, Blockchain, and Public-Interest Innovation

**Version:** 1.0.0  
**Document ID:** WIBC-CRAI-005  
**Status:** Foundational Partnership Framework  
**Maintained by:** Women in Blockchain Canada

# Purpose

The Canadian Research Partnership Ecosystem establishes a structured framework for forming, evaluating, governing, and sustaining collaborations that support responsible artificial intelligence, blockchain, Web4, cybersecurity, digital identity, decentralized education, commercialization, and public-interest technology.

The framework is designed to help researchers, universities, colleges, nonprofit organizations, governments, companies, community organizations, and international collaborators build grant-ready partnerships with clearly defined roles, shared responsibilities, transparent governance, and measurable outcomes.

---

# Strategic Importance

Strong research partnerships improve:

- scientific quality;
- funding competitiveness;
- implementation capacity;
- access to specialized expertise;
- community relevance;
- commercialization potential;
- public trust;
- policy alignment;
- long-term sustainability.

Partnerships should not be treated as names added to a proposal. Each partner should contribute a defined capability, resource, responsibility, or pathway to impact.

---

# Partnership Objectives

The framework seeks to:

- connect complementary institutions;
- strengthen multidisciplinary research;
- improve grant readiness;
- support equitable participation;
- reduce duplication;
- expand access to infrastructure;
- improve knowledge mobilization;
- accelerate responsible commercialization;
- strengthen Canadian research capacity;
- support international collaboration.

# Partnership Categories

## Academic Partners

Academic partners may include:

- universities;
- colleges;
- research institutes;
- laboratories;
- faculty members;
- graduate researchers;
- interdisciplinary centres.

Academic partners may contribute research leadership, methodology, ethics review, student training, facilities, publications, and technical expertise.

---

## Government Partners

Government partners may include:

- federal departments;
- provincial ministries;
- territorial governments;
- municipalities;
- Crown corporations;
- public agencies;
- regulatory bodies.

Government partners may contribute policy alignment, public datasets, procurement pathways, regulatory guidance, program funding, and implementation environments.

---

## Industry Partners

Industry partners may include:

- startups;
- small and medium-sized enterprises;
- large corporations;
- technology providers;
- financial institutions;
- consulting firms;
- commercialization partners.

Industry partners may contribute technical infrastructure, market knowledge, deployment environments, matching funds, product development, and commercialization pathways.

---

## Nonprofit and Community Partners

These may include:

- nonprofit organizations;
- charities;
- professional associations;
- advocacy organizations;
- community groups;
- workforce organizations;
- public-interest technology organizations.

They may contribute community access, lived experience, program delivery, beneficiary engagement, public trust, and impact measurement.

---

## Indigenous Partners

Indigenous partnerships should be built through respect, consent, reciprocity, and recognition of Indigenous data governance and community priorities.

Potential partners may include:

- First Nations;
- Inuit organizations;
- Métis organizations;
- Indigenous research institutes;
- community governments;
- Indigenous-owned enterprises.

Partnerships should not be extractive and should include shared decision-making, benefit, attribution, and appropriate stewardship of knowledge and data.

---

## International Partners

International partners may include:

- universities;
- research networks;
- standards organizations;
- development agencies;
- multilateral institutions;
- foundations;
- multinational companies;
- international nonprofits.

International collaboration should strengthen Canadian capacity while preserving data governance, intellectual property, legal compliance, and equitable participation.

# Partnership Lifecycle

## Stage 1 — Need Identification

Define the capabilities, resources, jurisdictions, communities, or implementation environments required by the project.

---

## Stage 2 — Partner Discovery

Identify organizations with relevant expertise, infrastructure, mission alignment, and delivery capacity.

---

## Stage 3 — Fit Assessment

Evaluate:

- strategic alignment;
- complementary capabilities;
- organizational credibility;
- resource availability;
- governance maturity;
- funding eligibility;
- conflict-of-interest risks;
- long-term commitment.

---

## Stage 4 — Role Definition

Document each partner’s:

- responsibilities;
- deliverables;
- personnel;
- financial contribution;
- in-kind contribution;
- decision authority;
- reporting obligations;
- ownership interests.

---

## Stage 5 — Agreement Development

Establish the formal relationship through appropriate documents such as:

- letters of support;
- memoranda of understanding;
- collaboration agreements;
- research agreements;
- data-sharing agreements;
- intellectual property agreements;
- subcontractor agreements;
- funding agreements.

---

## Stage 6 — Joint Proposal Development

Partners should collaborate on:

- research objectives;
- methodology;
- work packages;
- budget;
- governance;
- risk management;
- knowledge mobilization;
- impact measurement.

---

## Stage 7 — Implementation Governance

During implementation, partners should maintain:

- regular meetings;
- milestone tracking;
- issue escalation;
- documentation;
- financial oversight;
- responsible AI review;
- change control.

---

## Stage 8 — Evaluation and Continuation

At project completion, partners should assess:

- achievement of objectives;
- partnership effectiveness;
- research outputs;
- community impact;
- commercialization progress;
- future funding potential;
- continuation or expansion.

---

# Partner Evaluation Criteria

Potential partners should be assessed across the following domains:

- mission alignment;
- technical expertise;
- research credibility;
- implementation capacity;
- funding eligibility;
- governance maturity;
- data stewardship;
- ethical alignment;
- community trust;
- commercialization capability;
- international reach;
- long-term sustainability.

---

# Partnership Readiness Levels

## Level 1 — Exploratory

Initial discussion has occurred, but roles and commitments are not defined.

## Level 2 — Interested

The partner has expressed interest and identified a possible contribution.

## Level 3 — Proposal Ready

Roles, contributions, and deliverables are substantially defined.

## Level 4 — Agreement Ready

Formal documentation and approvals are in development or complete.

## Level 5 — Implementation Ready

The partnership has confirmed personnel, governance, resources, timelines, and reporting procedures.

---

# Governance Requirements

Every major partnership should define:

- project leadership;
- voting or decision rights;
- meeting cadence;
- reporting structure;
- approval thresholds;
- dispute resolution;
- change management;
- data governance;
- intellectual property;
- publication rights;
- termination procedures.

---

# Responsible AI Partnership Requirements

Partners involved in AI development or deployment should agree on:

- human oversight;
- privacy;
- consent;
- cybersecurity;
- fairness;
- accessibility;
- explainability;
- auditability;
- model monitoring;
- incident response;
- responsible disclosure.

---

# Data Governance

Partnership agreements should address:

- data ownership;
- data access;
- data storage;
- data residency;
- privacy obligations;
- security controls;
- retention;
- deletion;
- licensing;
- permitted reuse;
- publication.

---

# Intellectual Property

Intellectual property terms should clarify:

- background intellectual property;
- newly created intellectual property;
- licensing rights;
- commercialization rights;
- publication rights;
- attribution;
- patent responsibilities;
- open-source obligations;
- revenue-sharing arrangements.

---

# Equity and Inclusion

Partnerships should seek meaningful inclusion of:

- women;
- underrepresented researchers;
- early-career researchers;
- students;
- Indigenous communities;
- rural and remote communities;
- persons with disabilities;
- culturally diverse communities.

Inclusion should be reflected in governance, budgets, research roles, training opportunities, and benefit distribution.

---

# Knowledge Mobilization

Partners should define how findings will be shared through:

- academic publications;
- datasets;
- workshops;
- policy briefs;
- educational modules;
- open-source tools;
- public reports;
- standards submissions;
- community events;
- commercialization channels.

---

# Partnership Risk Categories

Common risks include:

- unclear roles;
- weak governance;
- delayed approvals;
- insufficient resources;
- conflicting objectives;
- data-access restrictions;
- intellectual-property disputes;
- partner withdrawal;
- unequal benefit;
- reputational concerns;
- regulatory noncompliance;
- cybersecurity incidents.

Each major risk should have an assigned owner and mitigation plan.

# Long-Term Objective

The Canadian Research Partnership Ecosystem provides a repeatable model for building high-quality collaborations that transform responsible AI and blockchain research into funded, governed, measurable, and scalable programs.

The framework is intended to strengthen Canada’s national innovation capacity while supporting equitable international adaptation and long-term public benefit.
