# Purpose

The Global Responsible AI Grant Standard establishes an internationally adaptable framework for identifying, evaluating, funding, governing, implementing, and measuring responsible artificial intelligence initiatives.

It synthesizes the Canadian Responsible AI Grant Standard, the AI Grant Strategy Series, responsible AI governance principles, commercialization pathways, partnership models, implementation controls, and inclusive innovation practices into a reusable global reference architecture.

The standard is designed for governments, universities, nonprofit organizations, research institutes, startups, corporations, foundations, multilateral institutions, and public-interest technology networks.

# Global Funding Lifecycle

## Stage 1 — Problem Definition

Define the social, scientific, technical, economic, educational, or institutional problem.

Required outputs may include:

- problem statement;
- beneficiary analysis;
- evidence summary;
- jurisdictional context;
- preliminary risk assessment.

---

## Stage 2 — Research Design

Develop the research questions, methodology, evidence plan, evaluation criteria, and expected contribution.

Required outputs may include:

- research protocol;
- literature review;
- methodology;
- inclusion criteria;
- ethics considerations;
- reproducibility plan.

---

## Stage 3 — Partnership Development

Establish the academic, technical, governmental, community, nonprofit, commercial, and international partnerships required for delivery.

Required outputs may include:

- stakeholder map;
- partner capability matrix;
- role descriptions;
- letters of support;
- governance agreements.

---

## Stage 4 — Funding Alignment

Identify funding programs aligned with the project’s objectives, jurisdiction, readiness, and public value.

Required outputs may include:

- funding opportunity matrix;
- eligibility review;
- deadline schedule;
- partnership requirements;
- funding-fit score.

---

## Stage 5 — Proposal Development

Prepare the complete funding proposal.

Required components may include:

- objectives;
- theory of change;
- methodology;
- work plan;
- budget;
- governance;
- responsible AI plan;
- risk register;
- impact framework;
- knowledge mobilization plan.

---

## Stage 6 — Responsible AI Review

Assess whether the proposed project adequately addresses:

- safety;
- fairness;
- privacy;
- security;
- accessibility;
- accountability;
- explainability;
- data governance;
- human oversight;
- lifecycle monitoring.

---

## Stage 7 — Implementation

Deliver the project under documented milestones, controls, decision gates, reporting requirements, and stakeholder oversight.

---

## Stage 8 — Evaluation

Measure:

- research quality;
- implementation performance;
- public benefit;
- institutional impact;
- commercialization progress;
- educational outcomes;
- governance effectiveness;
- sustainability.

---

## Stage 9 — Publication and Knowledge Mobilization

Publish appropriate outputs through:

- peer-reviewed papers;
- white papers;
- open datasets;
- implementation guides;
- educational resources;
- standards submissions;
- Zenodo;
- Figshare;
- institutional repositories.

---

## Stage 10 — Scale and Stewardship

Determine whether the project should be:

- expanded;
- replicated;
- licensed;
- standardized;
- transferred;
- institutionalized;
- maintained as a public good;
- retired.

# Standards Crosswalk

The global framework may be mapped against recognized standards, policies, and principles.

Mappings should distinguish among:

- direct equivalence;
- partial alignment;
- conceptual similarity;
- complementary requirements;
- implementation guidance;
- unresolved divergence.

A crosswalk must not imply formal adoption, endorsement, or direct influence without documentary evidence.

6. structured stakeholder evidence;
7. reputable industry research;

9. LLM-assisted synthesis subject to human verification.

---

# Historical Precedence and Influence Claims

The standard distinguishes between:

## Historical Precedence

A framework or publication predates a later source containing similar concepts.

## Conceptual Alignment

Two sources independently express similar principles or methods.

## Documented Influence

A later source explicitly cites, references, adopts, or attributes concepts to an earlier source.

## Formal Adoption

An institution officially incorporates a framework into a standard, policy, program, or operating procedure.

Historical precedence and conceptual alignment should not be represented as documented influence or formal adoption without evidence.

# Versioning

The standard should use semantic versioning:

- major version for structural or methodological changes;
- minor version for new datasets or framework extensions;
- patch version for corrections, metadata improvements, or citation updates.

Every release should include:

- version number;
- release date;
- change log;
- citation information;
- license;
- provenance statement.

# Zenodo and Figshare Strategy

Zenodo should be used for:

- DOI-backed release records.

# Limitations

The framework recognizes that:

- laws and policies change;
- funding programs expire;
- standards evolve;
- national contexts differ;
- data availability varies;
- implementation capacity is uneven;
- LLM-assisted analysis may contain errors;
- consensus does not eliminate uncertainty.

Limitations should be documented openly and updated as evidence changes.

# Long-Term Objective

The Global Responsible AI Grant Standard is intended to become a reusable international reference for converting responsible AI research into funded, governed, measurable, and scalable innovation.

Its long-term objective is to support a global ecosystem in which research, funding, governance, implementation, education, commercialization, and public benefit operate as an integrated and transparent system.
